Press and hold the Flex pad (or Space) to close the hand. Hold the grip in the green band to lift it.

What's going on?

Myoelectric prostheses read the tiny electrical signal (EMG) your muscles make when they contract. The raw signal is noisy, so it's rectified and smoothed into an envelope. Anything above a threshold drives the motors in proportion to how hard you flex. A longer smoothing window means a calmer signal but a laggier hand.

Most prosthetic hands give no sense of touch, so users grip by eye. Turn off haptic feedback to feel how hard that is.

What's going on?

The joint curves follow the classic shape of healthy walking. A passive prosthetic knee stays straight while it carries weight and the carbon foot can't push off, so the body vaults over a stiff leg and the other side works harder. Powered knees and ankles add motors to put that push-off back.

The energy and symmetry numbers are illustrative, based on typical ranges in gait research.

Move your cursor or drag on the stage. The arm solves inverse kinematics every frame.

What's going on?

The arm uses FABRIK, an iterative inverse kinematics solver, to find joint angles that put the gripper on your target. Each motor is speed limited, so it can't teleport. The bars show how much torque each joint needs just to hold the arm and payload against gravity, which is why the base motor is always the biggest.
